How to get a cross filter or star filter look, and which photos it works on

A star filter turns small, bright points of light into crossed rays. You can get the look with a glass filter on a lens, or digitally afterwards. Either way the picture decides how good it looks: it needs small bright spots against something darker.

What the filter actually does

A cross or star filter is clear glass with very fine parallel lines cut into it. Those lines spread light sideways, across their own direction, so a bright point gets a thin ray on each side. One set of lines gives two rays. Two sets crossing at a right angle give the classic four-point star. Three sets give six points and four sets give eight. Turning the filter on the lens turns the rays.

Only strong points of light make visible rays. The rest of the scene passes through almost unchanged, though the filter may soften it slightly. That is why the look seems to pick out the sparkle in a picture by itself.

How to shoot for it

  • Keep the background dark. Rays show best against shadow. Move so that the lights sit in front of something dark, not a bright sky.
  • Expose a little under. On a phone, tap a light and drag the brightness down a bit. Highlights stay small, and the scene around them keeps some depth.
  • Keep the lights in focus. A sharp small point makes a sharp ray.
  • Hold still at night. A shaky shot smears each light into a streak. Lean on something, or use a tripod.
  • Use a hard light for jewelry. Direct sun or a single small lamp gives crisp glints, where a big window gives a soft sheen. Move the piece slightly on video, and the glints will come and go on their own.

The three ways to get the look

A real glass filter. It screws onto a camera lens. The rays appear in the viewfinder and you turn the filter to place them. The result is baked into the picture, so you cannot soften it later.

A narrow aperture, for natural sunstars. With no filter at all, stopping a lens down to around f/11 to f/22 can turn the sun or a street lamp into a star. The number of points comes from the blades in the lens. An even number of blades gives that many points, and an odd number gives twice as many. Most phone lenses have a fixed aperture, so this one is mainly for cameras.

Digitally, afterwards. You can change your mind about strength and direction, and try it on pictures you already have. It needs the same small highlights that a filter would. Our free star filter tool does this on one photo in your browser, with nothing uploaded.

Settings that usually work

  • A few separate lights: four points at 45 degrees, longer rays, Strength around the middle.
  • Crowded crystal or sequins: lower Strength and lower Sensitivity, or the picture turns white.
  • Jewelry: shorter rays, six or eight points, some glitter.
  • Nothing happens: raise Sensitivity. If there is still nothing, the picture has no highlights to work from.
  • A straight-up look: set Angle to 0 for rays along the horizontal and vertical. Tilted rays usually look more natural.

Less is usually better. A good starting routine is to raise the effect until it is obviously too much, then bring it back until you stop noticing the sliders and start noticing the picture.
